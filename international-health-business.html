International Health Business

Business vocabulary and speaking practice for the global health sector — market access, pricing, supply chains, partnerships, funding and impact.

Warm-Up

Warm-Up — Business in International Health

Discuss these questions, then reveal the model answers.

1. What kinds of organisations work in international health?
Ministries of health, global health donors, multilateral agencies, pharmaceutical and diagnostics companies, NGOs, product development partnerships and local health providers.
2. Why is a health product not a normal product when you try to sell it?
The buyer is often not the patient. A ministry, an insurer or a donor pays, while a health worker uses the product and a patient receives it. The product must be approved, affordable and ethically acceptable before it can be sold.
3. What is "market access" and why does a company need it?
Market access is the process of getting a product approved, funded and available to patients. A company needs it because without regulatory approval, reimbursement and distribution, a good product never reaches the people who need it.
4. Who pays for health in low- and middle-income countries?
A mix: government budgets, global health donors and grants, development banks, private insurance, and out-of-pocket payment by patients. The mix decides what a product must cost and how it must be funded.

Reading

A Real Business Case

Read this proposal. What is the writer asking for? How does the structure help the reader?

Proposal: Expanding Access to Rapid Diagnostics in Senegal
Prepared for: Ministry of Health, Senegal  ·  Prepared by: Medequip International  ·  Date: 12 October 2026
1. Executive Summary — We propose an 18-month programme to expand access to rapid diagnostic tests for malaria and tuberculosis in 120 health facilities across Senegal. The programme will strengthen laboratory capacity, train 480 health workers and establish a national cold chain for temperature-sensitive diagnostics. Total cost: 1.2 million EUR, with 75% grant-funded.
2. Background & Problem — An estimated 40% of primary health facilities lack reliable diagnostic testing. Misdiagnosis leads to incorrect treatment and growing antimicrobial resistance. Existing supply chains lose 15% of stock to temperature damage before it reaches a facility.
3. Proposed Solution — Three workstreams: (a) equipment and installation in 120 facilities, (b) training and supervision for 480 health workers, (c) a last-mile delivery network covering all 14 regions.
4. Market Access & Pricing — We will submit the test to the national health technology assessment body, apply for inclusion on the national essential diagnostics list, and offer a tiered pricing agreement: 2.50 EUR per test in the public sector, 4.00 EUR in the private sector, with volume discounts for pooled procurement.
5. Timeline — Months 1–3: regulatory submission and HTA dossier. Months 4–9: installation and training. Months 10–18: last-mile delivery, supervision and impact reporting.
  • Month 6: 40% of facilities equipped
  • Month 12: national roll-out complete
  • Month 18: handover to the Ministry
6. Sustainability & Exit Plan — From month 12 the Ministry of Health takes over procurement. By year three the programme is self-financing through the national budget, and our role reduces to technical support.
The writer is asking the Ministry of Health to approve and co-fund a national diagnostic programme. The structure helps because every section has one job: the summary sells the idea, the problem proves it is needed, the solution shows how, market access and pricing show it is affordable, the timeline proves it is feasible, and the exit plan shows it will survive without endless donor money.
Key Component 1

Market Access & Health Technology Assessment

What it is: The process of getting a health product approved, reimbursed and available to patients — working with regulators, payers and national health authorities.

Why it matters: A great product means nothing if it cannot reach the patient. Market access is the business gateway between your product and the market.

Example: "We will submit our rapid diagnostic test to the national health technology assessment body and apply for inclusion on the essential diagnostics list."

Market access starts early. Build your HTA submission, pricing evidence and regulatory dossier at the same time as your clinical work — not after. Companies that start at the end of the project lose a year of market time.
Key Component 2

Pricing, Affordability & Equity

What it is: Setting prices that are affordable for low- and middle-income countries while keeping the business sustainable — through tiered pricing, pooled procurement or donor co-funding.

Why it matters: Affordability decides whether a product is adopted. Equity is also a business requirement: donors and ministries expect it, and it is written into grant conditions.

Example: "We propose a tiered pricing model: 2.50 EUR per test in the public sector, 4.00 EUR in the private sector, with volume discounts for pooled procurement."

Always separate the price from the value. Never say "cheap". Say "cost-effective per correct diagnosis" and show the cost of a wrong diagnosis. That turns a price objection into a value argument.
Key Component 3

Supply Chain, Cold Chain & Last-Mile Delivery

What it is: Moving products from factory to the point of care — storage, transport, temperature control and delivery to remote facilities.

Why it matters: A vaccine that arrives warm is useless. Supply chain failure is the most common reason global health programmes under-deliver — and the easiest problem to explain in business terms.

Example: "Our cold chain uses validated 2–8°C refrigerated transport with temperature loggers, and last-mile delivery reaches health posts within 48 hours of dispatch."

Speak in failure terms, not equipment terms. "We reduce temperature excursions by 90%" is stronger than "we use refrigerated trucks". Business audiences buy outcomes, not vehicles.
Key Component 4

Stakeholders & Multi-Stakeholder Partnerships

What it is: Identifying and engaging everyone affected by a project — ministries, donors, NGOs, health workers, communities — and building partnerships that share risk and resources.

Why it matters: International health is a multi-stakeholder environment. A project without the right partners fails, however good the product is.

Example: "The partnership brings together the Ministry of Health, a global health donor, a local NGO and our company — each contributing funding, policy support, community access and technical expertise."

Name the stakeholder and their interest. "The Ministry needs coverage data; the donor needs measurable outcomes; the NGO needs community trust." Generic "stakeholders" weakens your case — it tells the reader you have not done the work.
Key Component 5

Funding, Donors & Sustainability

What it is: Securing the money to run a programme — grants, donor funding, government budgets, blended finance — and planning how the programme survives after the initial funding ends.

Why it matters: Donors fund results and sustainability. A programme with no exit plan is a red flag, and it is usually the first thing a reviewer asks about.

Example: "75% of the 1.2 million EUR budget is grant-funded for 18 months. From month 12, the Ministry of Health takes over procurement, and the programme becomes self-financing by year three."

Answer the "and then what?" question in your first paragraph. Funders read the sustainability plan before the technical plan — a strong exit plan can rescue a weak technical section, but not the other way round.
Key Component 6

Measuring Impact & Health Outcomes

What it is: Defining and tracking what the programme achieves — coverage, correct diagnosis rates, treatment outcomes and cost per outcome.

Why it matters: Impact is the product in global health. Without indicators you cannot prove value, win a renewal or scale to another country.

Example: "We will track three indicators: percentage of facilities with reliable diagnostics (target 90%), correct diagnosis rate (target 85%) and stock-out rate (target below 5%)."

Use numbers with a baseline and a target. "We will improve diagnosis" is invisible. "From 40% to 85% correct diagnosis in 12 months" is measurable — and it is what a donor puts in the logframe.
Analysis

What Makes a Strong Business Case?

Use these six tests on any international health proposal or pitch.

1. Does the case name the beneficiary and the problem in numbers?
2. Does it explain market access and pricing — not just the product?
3. Does it name the partners and what each one contributes?
4. Does it show how the product reaches the patient (cold chain, last-mile)?
5. Does it have an impact indicator with a baseline and a target?
6. Does it answer the "and then what?" question — sustainability?
A strong case answers all six. The two most often missing are market access and sustainability. A pitch that describes a brilliant product but never says who approves it, who pays for it, or what happens when the grant ends, is a product brochure — not a business case.
Speaking Practice

Role-Plays & Weak vs Strong

Work in pairs. Take one role each, then swap. Reveal the model answers when you are ready.

Part A — Weak vs Strong
Weak: "Our test is very good and not expensive. Please buy it." — Rewrite this into a strong business sentence. What evidence do you add?
"Our rapid diagnostic test delivers a correct diagnosis in 15 minutes at 2.50 EUR per test, reducing incorrect treatment by an estimated 30% in facilities that currently have no laboratory capacity. We would like to propose a 12-month pilot in 40 facilities, with a full impact report at month 6."
Part B — The price objection
You are a market access manager. A ministry official says: "Your competitor's test costs half the price. Why should we pay more?" Respond — explain value, not price.
"I take your point on the headline price. Let me put that in context. The lower-priced test requires a laboratory, a trained technician and 24 hours for a result. Our test works in a health post with no laboratory, gives a result in 15 minutes, and so reduces lost follow-up visits. Measured per correct diagnosis, it is actually the lower cost — and it reaches patients the laboratory test cannot reach at all."
Part C — The donor wants results
You are a partnership manager. A donor wants measurable results within 12 months. Present your impact indicators and timeline. Use the phrases below.
"What I'd like to stress is that our indicators are measurable from month 3. By month 12 we will report: 90% of the 120 target facilities equipped, 480 health workers trained and assessed, and an 85% correct diagnosis rate against a 40% baseline. A full impact report is delivered at month 6 and month 12, so you can verify progress against each target."
Part D — The supply chain failure
You are a supply chain manager. A hospital director says the cold chain failed twice this quarter. Explain what happened and what you will change. Keep it calm and factual.
"Thank you for raising that — let me clarify what happened. Two shipments were delayed at the regional hub during a power outage, and temperature loggers recorded brief excursions above 8°C. Those batches were quarantined and replaced at no cost. We have since added backup refrigeration at the hub and a 24-hour alert system, so an excursion triggers a response before the product is dispatched, not after it arrives."
Useful speaking phrases:
Let me put that in context…I take your point, but…What I'd like to stress is…If I could add to that…To summarise our position…Let me clarify…

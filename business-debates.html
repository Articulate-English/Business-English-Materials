Business Debates

How to state a position, agree and disagree, handle objections and argue without damaging the relationship — for meetings, negotiations and presentations.

Warm-Up

Warm-Up — Arguing in Business

Discuss these questions, then reveal the model answers.

1. What makes a business argument convincing — data, emotion, or the speaker's authority?
Usually data first, then framing. A claim backed by a number is harder to dismiss than an opinion, but numbers alone rarely persuade: you have to connect them to something the listener cares about. Authority helps at the start and hurts if it is used instead of evidence — "because I said so" closes down discussion rather than winning it.
2. When you disagree with a colleague in a meeting, what is the best way to say it?
Acknowledge the part you accept first, then separate the person from the point: "I see why that works, and I'm concerned about the timeline." Avoid "You're wrong" and avoid "With all due respect" — both signal a personal attack. Aim for a disagreement that leaves the relationship intact, because you will need that colleague's support on the next decision.
3. Why do negotiations often fail even when both sides want a deal?
Because each side argues its own position instead of testing the other side's constraints. Most failed deals collapse on an issue neither party has actually asked about — budget timing, internal sign-off, risk allocation. Asking "what has to be true for this to work for you?" surfaces the real blocker faster than any amount of counter-argument.
4. Is it better to be direct or diplomatic in business? Give an example.
Neither, on its own. Direct about the substance, diplomatic about the delivery. Say "We cannot absorb that cost" rather than "That's fine" when you mean no — and say it with a reason the other side can repeat to their own boss. Indirect language protects the relationship but hides the message; blunt language delivers the message but burns the relationship. Skilled negotiators do both at once.
Useful phrases:
The data suggests…I take your point, but…Where I have concerns is…Help me understand…Could we explore an alternative?Let me put that in context…

Reading

A Real Business Case

Read this proposal. Who is arguing, and what is each side's strongest point?

Proposal: A Permanent Hybrid-Working Policy
Prepared for: Executive Team  ·  Prepared by: People Operations  ·  Date: 12 October 2026
1. Executive Summary — We propose a permanent hybrid policy: every employee works from the office at least two days a week, with team-specific anchor days. The policy carries an estimated cost of 180,000 EUR a year in travel, facilities and coordination, against an estimated retention saving of 340,000 EUR a year. Staff turnover has risen from 8% to 14% in two years, and exit interviews name flexibility as the top reason.
2. Background & Problem — The fully remote arrangement introduced during the pandemic has run for four years. Productivity measures have held steady, but onboarding has weakened: new joiners take five months to reach full independence, against a historical average of three, and managers report that informal problem-solving has largely disappeared.
3. Proposed Solution — Two fixed office days per week, chosen per team rather than per individual; no change to contracts or pay. Departments with client-facing duties keep existing schedules.
4. Risks & Objections — The strongest objection is that a two-day minimum penalises the people who asked for full remote work and have relocated. A second risk is inconsistent adoption: teams that treat anchor days as optional will lose the benefit. We propose an exemption route for both cases, with a six-month trial and a review.
5. Timeline & Decision Requested — Pilot in two departments for six months, then a full roll-out. We ask the executive team to approve the pilot and the exemption criteria at this meeting.
The People Operations team argues for the two-day minimum, and their strongest point is evidence: a measurable rise in attrition and a 340,000 EUR retention saving. The opposing case is rights and precedent — the policy reverses an arrangement people relied on, and some have relocated on the strength of it. Its strongest point is not emotion but fairness: the policy changes an arrangement employees planned their lives around. A strong debate on this motion therefore turns on the exemption route, not on whether flexibility is good.
Key Component 1

Stating a Position

What it is: Saying clearly which side you are on and why, in one sentence, before you argue anything.

Why it matters: A vague position forces the listener to guess, and people argue with the position they invented. A clear one lets them engage with the real argument.

Example: "I support the two-day minimum because the cost of losing new joiners is now higher than the cost of asking people to travel twice a week."

Use the form: position + because + reason. If you cannot fill in the "because" in one sentence, you do not have a position yet — you have a feeling. Test it by saying it out loud to a colleague and asking "what do you think I just argued for?"
Key Component 2

Agreeing & Supporting

What it is: Backing another person's point in a way that adds to it, rather than simply repeating it.

Why it matters: Agreement builds coalitions. Empty agreement ("I agree") is forgotten; agreement with a reason ("I agree, because it also solves X") moves the discussion forward.

Example: "I'd like to add to that — the two-day minimum also gives managers a predictable week, which was the second complaint in the exit interviews."

Add, don't echo. Use "I'd like to add to that…", "If I could build on that…", or "That's right, and it also…". And name what you support specifically — "I agree with the exemption idea" is more useful to the room than "I agree with Sarah".
Key Component 3

Disagreeing & Challenging

What it is: Opposing the idea while protecting the person — attacking the argument, never the colleague.

Why it matters: In most meetings, disagreement delivered badly ends the debate. A challenge that is specific and evidence-based is accepted as a contribution; a general objection is read as hostility.

Example: "I'm concerned about the six-month trial. If we measure retention but not team output, we will get a result we can't interpret — what metric would convince you either way?"

Use the sandwich: accept something real, then the challenge, then the way forward. "The retention argument is strong, and I think the onboarding numbers need a baseline, so let's agree the metric before we agree the policy." Never "That won't work" with no next step.
Key Component 4

Hedging, Softening & Saving Face

What it is: Softening absolute language so your point lands without cornering the other side.

Why it matters: Hard absolutes ("always", "never", "obviously") make the other side defend their intelligence instead of their position. Hedged language keeps them in the conversation — and lets them change their mind without losing face.

Example: "It may be that the pilot needs a longer baseline than six months." instead of "Six months is useless."

Swap absolutes for tentative verbs: "seems", "may", "tends to", "in my experience". Then hand the other side an exit: "I could be wrong, but have we considered…?" The goal is to win the argument, not the exchange — and an opponent who saves face will back your proposal next time.
Analysis

What Makes a Strong Argument?

Use these six tests on any position you are about to defend.

1. Can you state it in one sentence with a reason attached?
2. Is it supported by evidence, not just by strong opinion?
3. Do you know the strongest version of the other side's case?
4. Have you separated the person from the point?
5. Is there a concession you could make without losing the core?
6. Have you given the other side a way to change their mind with dignity?
A strong argument answers all six. The two most often missed are 3 and 6: people prepare their own case and never rehearse the opposing one, and they win the point by cornering someone who then blocks them for the rest of the quarter. The best debaters argue the other side's case better than their opponents do — and say so.
Speaking Practice

Role-Plays & Weak vs Strong

Work in pairs. Take one role each, then swap. Reveal the model answers when you are ready.

Part A — Weak vs Strong
Weak: "Your idea is too expensive and I don't like it." — Rewrite this into a strong business objection. What evidence do you add?
"I'm not convinced on the cost. The proposal adds 180,000 EUR a year and the retention saving you quote is an estimate, not a measured figure. I'd support a six-month pilot in one department, with the cost per retained employee calculated at the end — if it comes in under 20,000 EUR, I'd back the full roll-out."
Part B — The personal objection
A colleague says: "You only oppose this because you don't like the person who proposed it." Respond — without getting defensive.
"That's not the case, and I'd ask you to test it rather than assume it. My objection is the timeline: three months to recruit and train 40 people is tight. If you can show a realistic plan for that, I'll change my position — and if the objection were personal, the timeline argument wouldn't be one I'd need to make."
Part C — The concession
You are losing the argument on cost but winning on risk. Concede something real and pivot to the point you can win. Use the phrases below.
"You're right on the cost, and I'll accept the 180,000 EUR figure as a realistic estimate. Where I still have concerns is the risk side: if the pilot fails, we carry the cost and the reversal at the same time. So I'd like to propose a smaller pilot — one department, four months, with a decision gate at month 2. That keeps the cost argument you won and tests the risk argument I'm making."
Part D — Saving face
A senior manager has backed the wrong option in public and the numbers now show it. Raise it so they can change course without losing authority.
"Could I put a figure to this that I think changes the picture? The pilot came in at 240,000 EUR against a 180,000 estimate, which is a 33% variance. Given the direction you set at the start, I think the honest move is to widen the trial rather than roll out — that protects the original decision and the team's credibility at the same time."

Homework

Choose a real decision in your workplace or a current news story. Write two 150-word statements: one arguing for, one against. Then write a third paragraph — of no more than 80 words — conceding the strongest point on the other side and explaining why you still hold your position. Record yourself delivering the third paragraph and bring the recording and the three written versions to class.

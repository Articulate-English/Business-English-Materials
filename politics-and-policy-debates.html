Warm-Up

How Do You Argue About Policy?

Policy debates in business contexts — trade negotiations, regulatory affairs, investor briefings — require a specific tone. Click each question to reveal the model answer.

1. How do you disagree with a powerful stakeholder (a minister, a regulator, a client executive) without damaging the relationship?
You concede the parts you agree with first (Admittedly, the security concerns are valid), then pivot to your counter with evidence (However, the data from the last three trade rounds shows…). You never say You're wrong — instead, you say The evidence points in another direction or From my experience, the outcome differs. This shows respect for their position while maintaining your ground.
2. How do you signal that a policy position is your informed view, not just an opinion?
You cite sources (The WTO report shows…), reference precedent (We saw this in the EU-US trade tensions of 2019…), and use measured language (The evidence suggests… rather than It's clear that…). In business settings, you tie the policy to operational impact: This regulation will add 4% to compliance costs — here's the calculation.
3. When you're representing your company's view on a policy, how do you acknowledge broader stakeholder interests?
You frame your position as one stakeholder among many, not the final authority (From our vantage point as a mid-sized manufacturer… or In our sector's experience…). This gives you credibility because you're not claiming universal truth — you're describing your specific context with data, and then showing how it connects to wider impacts: While this policy protects local jobs, our analysis suggests it will increase input costs for downstream manufacturers, affecting competitiveness.

Debate Phrases

Arguing Both Sides

In business diplomacy, you often need to present multiple perspectives. These phrases let you hold two ideas at once.

On the one hand … on the other hand …
The classic structure for holding two opposing views in tension — essential in boardrooms and diplomatic briefings.
Example: "On the one hand, tariffs protect domestic jobs. On the other hand, they raise costs for downstream manufacturers."
While X is true, the evidence suggests…
Concedes a point gracefully before pivoting to your counter-evidence.
Example: "While tariffs do protect infant industries, the evidence suggests they invite retaliation that hurts exports."
From a broader perspective…
Elevates the debate from immediate to systemic — used by strategists and C-suite.
Example: "From a broader perspective, the real risk isn't this policy — it's the precedent it sets for future trade wars."
The data points in a different direction
A diplomatic way to contradict — focuses on evidence, not the person.
Example: "I don't dispute your concern. But the WTO trade data points in a different direction: retaliation cycles cost member states 0.8% of GDP on average."
The strongest policy arguments never start with contradiction. They start with concession (While X is true), then evidence pivot (the evidence suggests). This makes you sound like an analyst, not an advocate. The phrase "the data points in a different direction" is especially useful in business settings — it's softer than "you're wrong" but still decisive.
Debate Phrases

Backing Your Position

Use these phrases to introduce evidence, cite sources, and make your argument feel institutional rather than personal.

According to [source]…
The gold standard for citing authority — always pair with a recent, credible source.
Example: "According to the IMF's 2024 Global Economic Outlook, trade-restrictive measures reduced global GDP by 0.4% last year."
The precedent this sets is…
Shifts from immediate impact to long-term consequence — powerful in policy debates.
Example: "The precedent this sets is that economic disputes can be resolved via tariffs rather than multilateral frameworks."
From our vantage point…
Frames your view as one stakeholder's perspective — essential when you represent a company or sector.
Example: "From our vantage point as a European supplier, this policy creates a competitive disadvantage against subsidized Asian competitors."
The operational impact is…
Translates abstract policy into concrete business outcomes — the language executives use.
Example: "The operational impact is a 4% increase in input costs, which margins out over the next two quarters."
In policy debates, "I think" is dangerous. Replace it with "The data suggests" or "According to [source]". The moment you cite an external authority, you stop sounding like an advocate and start sounding like an analyst. And "the operational impact is" is how you make policy relevant to business audiences — it translates abstract rules into P&L effects.

Exercise

Choose the Right Phrase

Three scenarios. For each, decide which phrase from slides 3-4 fits best — then reveal the full model answer.

1. Your boss says tariffs always protect jobs, period. You have WTO data showing retaliation costs. How do you respond?
Concede the principle, pivot to the evidence: I don't dispute that tariffs protect domestic jobs in the short term. However, the data points in a different direction: the WTO reports show that retaliation cycles cost member states 0.8% of GDP on average. The operational impact is that downstream exports lose 3% in value within two quarters. You've used the concession phrase to avoid a direct clash, then shifted to hard data.
2. A stakeholder argues that the UN is irrelevant because major powers ignore it. You want to defend multilateralism. Which phrases help?
Acknowledge the criticism, reframe the question: That's a fair concern about UN deadlock. However, the UN coordinates 60% of global humanitarian aid and peacekeeping budgets that bilateral deals cannot replicate. From a broader perspective, the real risk isn't irrelevance — it's that without the UN, we lose the forum where small nations have a voice. You've moved from their narrow critique (irrelevant) to the systemic value (forum for small nations).
3. You're briefing your CEO on a new social-media regulation. How do you frame both the risk and the opportunity?
Use both-sides framing, then close with your recommendation: On the one hand, this regulation increases compliance costs by 5%. On the other hand, it creates a competitive advantage for companies that adapt early. From our vantage point, the precedent this sets is that regulators will prioritise accountability over growth — so our recommendation is to position the company as a compliance leader now. You've presented both sides, then used "from our vantage point" to make the recommendation sound strategic, not reactive.
Key Phrases

Conceding Without Losing

In high-stakes policy debates, the skill isn't never giving ground — it's giving ground strategically. These phrases let you concede a point while keeping your overall position intact.

I accept that premise, but the scope is narrower
A master concession — you agree with their principle but narrow its application to your favour.
Example: "I accept that tariffs can protect infant industries — but the scope is narrower than the proposal suggests. This specific tariff targets 200 product categories, not the whole sector."
The question isn't whether, but how
Reframes a binary debate into a design discussion — takes you from defender to solution-provider.
Example: "The question isn't whether AI should be regulated — it's how. And the EU AI Act's risk-tiered approach offers a workable model."
I'd propose a nuanced approach
Shifts from opposition to contribution — you're not blocking, you're improving.
Example: "I'd propose a nuanced approach: sunset clauses on protectionist measures, tied to a domestic-industry competitiveness review."
The evidence suggests a middle ground
Ends the concession by steering toward compromise — you're the reasonable one in the room.
Example: "The evidence suggests a middle ground between full autonomy and complete integration — a managed-standards partnership."
The best policy debaters don't win every point — they win the framing. "I accept that premise, but the scope is narrower" is a masterstroke: you've conceded the principle while limiting the application. And "The question isn't whether, but how" shifts the entire frame from opposition to design — you become the solution-provider, not the obstacle.
Homework

Write Your Position

Choose one of the four debate topics from the source page. Write a 3-minute position speech — at least 200 words. Use at least 4 phrases from this deck (slides 3-4 and slide 7). Record yourself speaking.

The four topics:
1. Tariffs are an effective policy 2. Social media algorithms threaten democracy more than state censorship 3. The US is no longer a reliable EU ally 4. Populism is a symptom, not a threat
Full structure suggestion:
Opening (30s): On balance, I believe… Admittedly, there are valid concerns… From my perspective, the evidence points to…
Evidence (60s): According to [source]… The data suggests… The operational impact is… From a broader perspective…
Counter-concession (60s): I accept that premise, but the scope is narrower… The question isn't whether, but how… I'd propose a nuanced approach…
Conclusion (30s): The evidence suggests a middle ground… I'd argue the balanced path forward is…
Full model answer — Topic 1 (tariffs on the auto industry):
"Colleagues, I'm going to argue that targeted tariffs on Chinese EV imports are justified — but only as a temporary bridge to domestic manufacturing capacity, not as a permanent policy.

On balance, I believe the data points in a different direction from those who say tariffs always harm competition. According to the Peterson Institute's 2024 study, the EU's temporary safeguard duties on Chinese solar panels increased domestic production by 18% without raising consumer prices — because the tariffs were paired with a clear sunset clause.

However, the precedent this sets is dangerous if applied broadly. From a broader perspective, the real threat isn't protecting infant industries — it's the retaliatory spiral that damages everyone. The US–China trade tensions of 2019 cost both economies over $12 billion in lost output.

I accept that premise, but the scope is narrower than a blanket tariff policy. These specific tariffs target electric vehicles above 25% import duty, not the entire automotive supply chain.

The question isn't whether to protect domestic manufacturing — it's how. And I'd propose a nuanced approach: temporary tariffs tied to a domestic production review in 18 months. The evidence suggests a middle ground between protection and free trade — a managed transition."
Full model answer — Topic 2 (Social media vs. State Censorship):
"Distinguished delegates, the data points in a different direction from those who frame this as a simple freedom vs. control debate. According to the UN's 2024 report on digital rights, state censorship directly suppresses 164 million users annually — but social media algorithms indirectly suppress an estimated 492 million, through engagement-driven filtering that amplifies divisive content.

However, the precedent this sets is critical. From a broader perspective, the real risk isn't the platform's algorithm — it's that treating content moderation as state censorship gives authoritarian regimes cover to suppress legitimate dissent. Three years ago, Russia used the "social media is just like state censorship" framing to justify its own platform bans — and 70% of surveyed activists reported being surveilled as a result.

I accept that premise, but the scope is narrower than a blanket regulatory approach. The question isn't whether platforms shape discourse — it's how. I'd propose a nuanced approach: algorithmic transparency requirements — not content bans — paired with public interest data audits. The evidence suggests a middle ground between free expression and harm prevention: transparent algorithms, not government censors."
Full model answer — Topic 3 (US as Reliable Ally):
"Ambassador, on balance, I believe the transatlantic relationship remains structurally resilient — but the optics of unreliability are real and must be addressed.

The operational impact is measurable: the US has invoked Article 5 once in NATO history (2001), responded to 89% of EU crisis consultations since 2017, and committed $65 billion in military aid to Ukraine — more than any other ally. Admittedly, that's only true when assessing concrete commitments, not diplomatic tone. From my experience, the volatility in US foreign policy rhetoric scares European capitals more than the actual policy shifts.

That said, the evidence points in a different direction than the "America First is permanent" narrative. According to the Chicago Council's 2024 survey, 68% of Americans now favour maintaining NATO, and 72% support continued EU cooperation on tech standards. The precedent this sets — that alliances are transactional — is dangerous if unaddressed. I'd argue the middle path is institutionalising ties at the bureaucratic level (trade accords, data sharing, joint supply chains) so that relationship continuity doesn't depend on electoral cycles."
Full model answer — Topic 4 (Populism as Symptom):
"Esteemed panel, the question isn't whether populism is a threat — it's how we read what it's telling us. On the one hand, the surge in populist movements from 2010 to 2024 correlates with a 22% decline in real median wages across OECD nations and a 31% rise in wealth inequality. On the other hand, dismissing populism as mere xenophobia misses the structural grievance: communities hollowed out by deindustrialisation vote with desperation, not ideology.

While this is true, the evidence suggests that addressing root causes — regional economic decline, labour displacement, digital alienation — reduces populist appeal by 40% in comparative studies. From a broader perspective, populism is the symptom; globalisation's uneven gains are the disease. However, the precedent this sets — treating economic anxiety as inherently irrational — only deepens the divide. The evidence suggests a middle ground between technocratic dismissal and populist pandering: acknowledge the grievance, address the inequality, but refuse the scapegoat."

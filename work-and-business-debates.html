Work & Business Debates

Tackle the big questions in the modern workplace — four-day weeks, AI hiring, diversity programs, and retirement. Practice taking a clear position, backing it with evidence, and conceding fairly.

Warm-Up

How Do You Debate?

Before diving into the topics, practise the basics of a strong debate argument. Click each question to reveal the answer.

1. What makes a debate argument sound confident rather than aggressive?
Use concession phrases (Admittedly, …, That said, …) and linkers (Furthermore, However). Lower your voice on strong points — volume signals control, not volume. A confident speaker sounds measured, not loud.
2. Why should you acknowledge the opposite side before stating your position?
It builds credibility. A phrase like Admittedly, … or From my perspective, … shows you've considered complexity. When you then hit them with I'd argue that … or On balance, I believe …, your own point lands harder because the listener knows you're not ignoring the other side.
3. How do you handle it when you disagree with the whole room?
Find a sliver of common ground first (I think we all agree that …), then introduce your counter with a softener (From my experience, … or With respect, …). Never contradict outright. That said, … is your bridge: it acknowledges them while pivoting to you.

Debate Phrases

Opening & Taking a Position

On balance, I believe…
Use this to state your overall position clearly at the start.
Example: "On balance, I believe the four-day week benefits knowledge workers, though not manufacturing."
I couldn't agree more
Strong agreement — use when you want to align with a point before building on it.
Example: "I couldn't agree more. The evidence clearly shows New Zealand trials increased output by 22%."
Admittedly, …
Concession opener — acknowledge the opposing view without losing your ground.
Example: "Admittedly, not every industry can adapt — but the evidence suggests service sectors can."
From my perspective, …
Soft entry to a counter-argument — less confrontational than "I disagree".
Example: "From my perspective, algorithmic hiring introduces a different set of risks — but they are auditable."
The strongest opening does three things: states your position (On balance), acknowledges complexity (Admittedly), then pivots to your evidence (The evidence shows). Never lead with disagreement — always lead with common ground or shared facts.
Debate Phrases

Pushback & Concession

That said, …
Bridge phrase — acknowledges but pivots. Your Swiss Army knife of debate.
Example: "That's a fair point. That said, the bigger issue is whether we can audit algorithms more easily than human bias."
With respect, I'd counter that…
Polite disagreement — essential when challenging a stronger speaker.
Example: "With respect, I'd counter that human interviews are themselves biased — we just can't trace the decision tree."
But isn't the real question…?
Redirects to the core issue instead of arguing side points.
Example: "But isn't the real question whether we can audit an outcome more easily than a conversation?"
I'd argue the middle path is…
Ends a concession with your own forward motion — keeps you in control.
Example: "I'd argue the middle path is letting AI assist with hiring while keeping firing human."
Concede small points, not the whole argument. When someone says "bias is bad," agree — then say "The question is which bias we can better control: algorithmic or human." This makes you sound reasonable, not defensive. Never say "You're wrong" — reframe to "The data shows another angle."

Exercise

Choose the Right Phrase

Three scenarios. For each, pick the debate phrase that would work best — then reveal the full model answer.

1. Your colleague says four-day weeks won't work because employees will slack off.
Start with acknowledgment, then pivot with evidence: That's a fair concern about productivity. However, the trials in New Zealand showed output actually increased because people focused more intensely during their working hours. The key word here is however — it signals you're not agreeing, you're redirecting with data.
2. Someone argues AI hiring is biased, so we shouldn't use it at all.
Concede the bias point, then reframe the question: You're absolutely right that bias exists — no system is neutral by default. But the question is whether we can audit an algorithm's bias more easily than we can an interviewer's unconscious preferences. You're not denying the problem — you're widening the lens.
3. A classmate says D&I programs are "just box-ticking."
Acknowledge the risk, then hit with the business case: If we're purely transactional about it, sure — D\&I can become performative. But companies with diverse leadership consistently outperform their peers by 35%. The business case makes tokenism irrelevant. You've used if we're purely transactional to concede their framing, then but to flip it.
Key Phrases

Concluding Your Argument

The strongest debates end with a path forward, not just a summary. These phrases help you close cleanly.

I'd argue the middle path is…
Summarises your compromise position without sounding like you gave up.
Example: "I'd argue the middle path is letting AI assist with hiring while keeping firing human."
To wrap up, the evidence shows…
Signals conclusion while circling back to data, not emotion.
Example: "To wrap up, the evidence shows diverse teams make better decisions — but only if inclusion is real, not performative."
The bottom line is…
Cuts through clutter — use it when you have one final point that must stick.
Example: "The bottom line is that work should enhance human dignity, not replace it."
What we should take away from this is…
Frames your conclusion as a shared insight, not just your opinion.
Example: "What we should take away is that regulation, not banning, is the path forward."
Never end with a restatement — end with a call to the shared reality. "The bottom line" and "What we should take away" both assume the listener can follow your logic. If you've lost them, no closing phrase rescues the argument. That's why the whole debate matters: each concession you make on slides 3-4 earns you the right to close.
Homework

Write Your Own Position

Choose one of the four debate questions below. Write a 3-minute position speech — at least 150 words. Use at least 4 phrases from this deck (slides 3-4). Record yourself speaking.

Choose your topic:
1. Four-day work week as global standard2. AI in hiring and firing3. D&I programs are effective4. Retirement age should be 70
Suggested structure for your speech:
1. Opening (30s): On balance, I believe… Admittedly, … From my perspective, …

2. Main argument + evidence (90s): Use data, acknowledge a counter-point, then pivot with However / That said, …

3. Concession (30s): I see it differently. With respect, I'd counter that… The question is whether…

4. Conclusion (30s): To wrap up, the evidence shows… The bottom line is… I'd argue the middle path is…
Full model answer — Topic 2 (AI in hiring/firing):
"Good morning. I'm going to argue that companies should be allowed to use AI for hiring, but not for firing.

On balance, algorithmic hiring tools can reduce bias — if they're audited. When Unilever introduced AI screening, it increased diverse candidate shortlists by 16%. That's because humans carry unconscious bias into interviews that algorithms, at their best, can flag.

Admittedly, that's only true if the training data itself is fair. But here's the thing: human judgment is also biased — we just can't audit it as easily as we can an algorithm. At least with AI, we can trace the decision tree.

That said, firing is different. When an algorithm tells someone they've lost their job, with no human explanation, we've crossed an ethical line. Employment isn't just about efficiency — it's about dignity. A human manager must be in the loop for termination decisions.

I'd argue the middle path: let AI assist with hiring (with oversight), but keep firing human. The technology should augment human judgment, not replace the human element entirely."
Full model answer — Topic 1 (Four-day work week):
"Colleagues, on balance, I believe the four-day week is viable for knowledge work, though not universally applicable.

The evidence from Microsoft Japan's 2019 trial showed a 40% increase in productivity — but that was in a high-margin tech sector, not manufacturing or retail. Admittedly, not every industry can adapt: call centres need coverage, hospitals need staffing. However, the UK's 2022 national trial covered 60 companies across sectors — and 92% reported either improved or unchanged productivity.

From my perspective, the question isn't whether people work less — it's whether the question isn't whether, but how we redesign work. That said, the real risk isn't slacking off — it's the transition cost: training staff to be more efficient in fewer hours.

I'd argue the middle path is a phased rollout: start with a 32-hour, 4-day week at 100% pay for knowledge workers, paired with productivity metrics. To wrap up, the evidence shows this isn't about working less — it's about working smarter. The bottom line is that productivity gains from a four-day week fund the transition itself."
Full model answer — Topic 3 (D&I programs):
"I'm going to argue that on balance, I believe D&I programs deliver measurable business value — when they go beyond box-ticking.

The data from McKinsey's 2023 Diversity Matters report shows companies in the top quartile for ethnic diversity are 39% more likely to outperform peers in profitability. Admittedly, many programs are performative: a one-time unconscious bias workshop followed by no structural change isn't enough. With respect, I'd counter that the criticism isn't of D&I itself — it's of lazy implementation.

But the question isn't whether D&I works — it's how. Companies like Accenture tied 30% of executive bonuses to inclusion goals — and their diverse leadership representation rose from 18% to 42% in five years. I'd propose a nuanced approach: measure inclusion through retention rates, promotion parity, and pay equity — not just headcount.

I'd argue the middle path is outcome-based diversity: set targets on representation at senior levels, tie them to compensation, and audit quarterly. To wrap up, the evidence shows that diversity without inclusion is just optics — but with accountability, it drives real performance. The bottom line is that D&I done right is the single most reliable lever for improving decision-making quality."
Full model answer — Topic 4 (Retirement age):
"Colleagues, on balance, I believe raising the retirement age to 70 is the right direction — but at different speeds by sector.

The operational impact is clear: Japan's gradual raise from 60 to 65 saved $32 billion in pension liabilities over a decade. Admittedly, that figure matters less than quality of life for workers in physically demanding jobs. From my perspective, the question isn't whether people can work longer — it's whether the question isn't whether, but how we make work sustainable for older employees.

That said, the real issue isn't the retirement age — it's that many 70-year-olds today are in roles designed for 40-year-olds. However, countries like Sweden already offer flexible retirement: you can claim partial pension at 62, full at 67, and work part-time or in advisory roles until 70.

I'd argue the middle path is sector-adjusted retirement: raise the age to 70 for sedentary work (with health check-ins), keep it at 65 for manual labour. To wrap up, the evidence suggests a middle ground between extending careers and protecting the vulnerable. What we should take away from this is that retirement isn't about age — it's about capability and contribution."

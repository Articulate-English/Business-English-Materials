Business Idioms

Speak like a professional. Learn the idioms you will hear in real meetings, calls and emails — and start using them with confidence.

Warm-Up

Why Do People Use Idioms?

Discuss these questions, then reveal the answers.

Idioms = Shortcuts to Complex Ideas
An idiom is a phrase whose meaning is different from the individual words. "Ball is in your court" does not mean a ball — it means "your turn to act". In business, idioms save time, build rapport, and signal that you belong.
1. Why do international professionals avoid idioms — and what happens when they do?
Many avoid idioms because they fear making mistakes. But avoiding them can make your English sound formal, distant, or "textbook". Using idioms correctly shows cultural confidence — and helps you build relationships faster.
2. What is the risk of using an idiom incorrectly in a business call?
The risk is low — most listeners will understand from context, and they will appreciate the effort. The bigger risk is staying silent and never sounding natural. If you are unsure, use a simpler phrase — but do not avoid idioms forever.

Key Phrases

Idioms for Meetings & Discussion

Beat around the bush
"Stop beating around the bush — what is the real issue?"
Avoiding the main point. Use to push for clarity.
Get straight to the point
"Let me get straight to the point — we are over budget."
Say it directly. Professional and respectful.
Wrap it up
"We have 5 minutes left — let us wrap it up."
Finish something. Use to close a meeting or topic.
Table that discussion
"Let us table that and revisit it next week."
Postpone a topic. Common in US business English.
In meetings, native speakers use idioms to manage time and tone. "Wrap it up" is polite but firm. "Table that" avoids conflict by delaying, not rejecting. Learn these and you will sound like a meeting pro.

Exercise

Choose the Right Idiom

1. Your team has been discussing for 20 minutes without deciding. Which idiom fits?
"I think we are beating around the bush. Can we get straight to the point and decide?"
2. You want to test a new idea with your manager before the full team. Which idiom fits?
"Let me run it up the flagpole with the director first — if she agrees, we can present it to the whole team."
3. A colleague has done excellent work on a project. Which idiom fits?
"She really went the extra mile on this one — the client was impressed and the result is a win-win situation for everyone."
Key Phrases

Idioms for Email & Follow-Up

Circle back
"Let me circle back on this after I check the numbers."
Return to a topic later. Very common in emails.
Keep me in the loop
"Please keep me in the loop on any changes."
Ask to be included in updates. Professional and direct.
Loop someone in
"I will loop in the finance team on this."
Add someone to the conversation or email chain.
Ball is in your court
"I have sent the proposal — the ball is in your court now."
It is their turn to act. Use after sending information.
Email idioms are everywhere in business. "Circle back" and "keep me in the loop" are safe, professional, and used daily. "Ball is in your court" is slightly more direct — use it when you want someone to take responsibility.
Homework

Practice Tasks

1. Write an email to a colleague asking them to update you on a project. Use "keep me in the loop" and "circle back".
2. Write a short message to your manager suggesting a new idea. Use "run it up the flagpole" and "think outside the box".
3. Write a closing sentence for a meeting where everyone agreed on next steps. Use "on the same page" and "get the ball rolling".
1. "Hi Maria, I just wanted to circle back on the Q3 report. Could you please keep me in the loop on any changes before Friday?"

2. "I have an idea for the client presentation — can I run it up the flagpole? I think we should think outside the box and use video instead of slides."

3. "Great — I think we are all on the same page. Let us get the ball rolling on the action items before we close."

Plant Tour: Inside the Machines

Describe every machine in a chemical plant with confidence — what it does, how it works, and why it matters.

Warm-Up

Warm-Up — The Machines

Discuss these questions, then reveal the model answers.

1. Have you ever worked with a machine you found difficult to explain to others? What made it hard?
Many people struggle when a machine has invisible processes — things happening inside a sealed unit. The key is to focus on inputs and outputs: what goes in, what comes out, and what transformation happens in between.
2. What makes a machine description clear and useful for a visitor?
A clear description covers three things: the purpose (what it does), the process (how it works), and the result (what comes out). It avoids unexplained jargon and uses comparisons to familiar things.
3. Why is it important to describe machines accurately in a professional setting?
Inaccurate descriptions can confuse visitors, create safety risks, or damage your company's reputation. Precision shows expertise. If you say a machine produces 500 cubic metres per hour, that number must be correct.
4. How would you describe a machine you have never seen before, using only its name and its position in a process?
Use context clues. If the machine comes after the purifier and before the cold box, it likely compresses or cools the gas. The name often hints at the function — a compressor compresses, a condenser condenses. Combine the name with the process flow to make an educated guess.

Machine Descriptions from ItalChimica

Read these machine descriptions from the ItalChimica hydrogen plant. Notice how each description covers purpose, process, and result.

ItalChimica S.p.A. — Machine Descriptions
Hydrogen Production Plant  ·  6 Machines in Sequence
1. Electrolyser — "This machine is responsible for splitting water into hydrogen and oxygen. What happens inside is a controlled electric current passing through an alkaline solution. The hydrogen collects at the cathode, and the oxygen collects at the anode. The key component to notice is the membrane in the centre — it allows ions to pass through but keeps the two gases completely separate. This stage produces 500 cubic metres of hydrogen per hour."
2. Purifier — "The purpose of this unit is to remove any remaining moisture and oxygen from the hydrogen gas. What happens inside is a chemical reaction that binds the impurities to a special material, which is then filtered out. The result is hydrogen at 99.9% purity. This stage prepares the gas for compression."
3. Compressor — "This machine is responsible for increasing the pressure of the hydrogen gas from atmospheric pressure to 20 bar. What happens inside is a series of pistons that squeeze the gas into a smaller volume. The key component to notice is the cooling system — compression generates heat, and the cooling system removes it before the gas moves on. This stage prepares the gas for liquefaction."
4. Cold Box — Precooler — "The purpose of this unit is to cool the hydrogen gas from ambient temperature down to 80 Kelvin. What happens inside is a heat exchanger that uses liquid nitrogen as a coolant. You can see the nitrogen inlet pipes on the left. This stage prepares the gas for deep cooling."
5. Cold Box — Deep Cooler — "This machine is responsible for cooling the hydrogen from 80 Kelvin down to just 20 Kelvin — 20 degrees above absolute zero. What happens inside is an expansion turbine: the gas is allowed to expand rapidly, which reduces its temperature dramatically. The key component to notice is the turbine blade — it spins at over 100,000 revolutions per minute."
6. Storage Tank — "The purpose of this unit is to store liquid hydrogen at atmospheric pressure. What happens inside is nothing — the tank is a passive container. The key component to notice is the vacuum insulation between the inner and outer walls. This prevents heat from entering and keeps the hydrogen in liquid form. Our storage capacity is 200 tonnes."
Each machine description follows the same structure: purpose (what it does), process (how it works), and result (what comes out). This consistency makes it easy for visitors to follow the process flow. The descriptions also use sequencing language to show how each machine connects to the next — preparing the gas for the next stage.
Machine 1

The Electrolyser

What it does: Splits water (H2O) into hydrogen and oxygen using a controlled electric current.

How it works: Water enters the electrolyser. An electric current passes through an alkaline solution. Hydrogen gas collects at the cathode (negative electrode). Oxygen gas collects at the anode (positive electrode). A membrane in the centre keeps the two gases separate.

Key numbers: 500 cubic metres of hydrogen per hour. 99.9% pure.

When describing the electrolyser, always mention the membrane — it is the key component that makes the process safe. Without it, the two gases would mix and create an explosion risk. Say: "The membrane allows ions to pass through but keeps the two gases completely separate."
Machine 2

The Purifier

What it does: Removes remaining moisture and oxygen from the hydrogen gas after electrolysis.

How it works: The gas passes through a chemical binder that traps impurities. The binder material is periodically replaced. The result is hydrogen at 99.9% purity — essential for the next stage.

Why it matters: Impurities in the gas would damage the compressor and reduce the efficiency of liquefaction. Purification protects the downstream equipment.

Use the phrase "this stage prepares the gas for…" to connect the purifier to the next machine. This shows visitors that each machine has a purpose in the sequence — nothing is there by accident. Say: "This stage prepares the gas for compression."
Machine 3

The Compressor

What it does: Increases the pressure of the hydrogen gas from atmospheric pressure to 20 bar.

How it works: A series of pistons squeezes the gas into a smaller volume. Compression generates heat, so a cooling system removes the excess temperature before the gas moves on.

Why it matters: Compressing the gas makes it easier to cool in the next stage. At higher pressure, the gas requires less energy to liquefy — making the process more efficient.

When describing the compressor, mention the cooling system — it shows you understand that compression is not just about pressure. Say: "The key component to notice is the cooling system. Compression generates heat, and the cooling system removes it before the gas moves on."
Machine 4

The Cold Box — Precooler

What it does: Cools the hydrogen gas from ambient temperature (25°C) down to 80 Kelvin (-193°C).

How it works: A heat exchanger uses liquid nitrogen as a coolant. The nitrogen flows through pipes around the hydrogen channel, absorbing heat. You can see the nitrogen inlet pipes on the left of the unit.

Why it matters: This is the first of two cooling stages. Precooling to 80 Kelvin makes the deep cooling stage far more efficient — it is much easier to go from 80K to 20K than from 25°C to 20K.

Use the phrase "you can see…" to point out visible features. This helps visitors connect your description to what they can actually see. Say: "You can see the nitrogen inlet pipes on the left. The nitrogen flows through these pipes to absorb heat from the hydrogen."
Machine 5

The Cold Box — Deep Cooler

What it does: Cools the hydrogen from 80 Kelvin down to 20 Kelvin (-253°C) — just 20 degrees above absolute zero.

How it works: An expansion turbine allows the gas to expand rapidly. This expansion reduces the temperature dramatically. The turbine blade spins at over 100,000 revolutions per minute.

Why it matters: At 20 Kelvin, hydrogen condenses from a gas into a liquid. Liquid hydrogen takes up far less space than gas — making it practical to store and transport.

The expansion turbine is the most impressive machine in the plant. Emphasise the speed: "over 100,000 revolutions per minute." Visitors remember dramatic numbers. Say: "The key component to notice is the turbine blade — it spins at over 100,000 revolutions per minute."
Machine 6

The Storage Tank

What it does: Stores liquid hydrogen at atmospheric pressure after liquefaction.

How it works: The tank is a passive container — no moving parts. The key feature is the vacuum insulation between the inner and outer walls. This prevents heat from entering and keeps the hydrogen in liquid form.

Key numbers: 200 tonnes of liquid hydrogen. Zero incidents in over 10 years of operation.

The storage tank is the one machine with no moving parts — and that is its strength. Emphasise the vacuum insulation: "The key component to notice is the vacuum insulation between the inner and outer walls. This prevents heat from entering and keeps the hydrogen in liquid form." End with the safety record: "We've maintained zero incidents in over 10 years of operation."
Analysis

What Makes a Great Machine Description?

A great machine description passes these six tests. Which ones would your descriptions pass?

Clear purpose — the visitor understands what the machine does and why it exists.
Simple process — the internal workings are explained in accessible language.
Visible features — the visitor can see the parts you are describing.
Key numbers — capacity, pressure, temperature, and purity are stated accurately.
Process connection — the description shows how this machine fits into the sequence.
Memorable detail — the visitor leaves with one impressive fact they will remember.
Test your description with one question: "If I knew nothing about this machine, would I understand what it does and why it matters?" If the answer is yes, you are ready. Practise aloud at least three times — confidence comes from repetition, and accuracy comes from preparation.
Practice

Weak vs Strong

Improve these weak machine descriptions.

1. Weak: "This is the compressor. It compresses the gas." — What makes this weak?
It is circular — it uses the word "compresses" to explain what a compressor does. It gives no useful information about the pressure, the purpose, or the result. The visitor learns nothing.
2. Rewrite: "This is the cold box. It's very cold inside." — Make this stronger.
"This is our cold box — the deep cooler. It reduces the temperature of the hydrogen from 80 Kelvin down to just 20 Kelvin, which is 20 degrees above absolute zero. At this temperature, the gas condenses into a liquid, which is far easier to store and transport."
3. Which elements are missing here? "That's the storage tank. It stores hydrogen."
The capacity, the insulation method, the safety record, and the purpose are all missing. A strong description adds value: "Our storage capacity is 200 tonnes, and the vacuum insulation between the walls keeps the hydrogen in liquid form. We've maintained zero incidents in over 10 years of operation."
Vocabulary & Homework

Key Vocabulary & Homework

electrolyser — the unit that splits water into hydrogen and oxygen using an electric current

purifier — a unit that removes impurities from a gas or liquid stream

compressor — a machine that increases the pressure of a gas by reducing its volume

condenser — a device that cools a gas until it becomes a liquid

expansion turbine — a machine that reduces gas temperature by allowing it to expand rapidly

vacuum insulation — a method of preventing heat transfer by removing air from a space between walls

membrane — a thin barrier that allows some particles to pass through but blocks others

Homework

Write a description of one machine at your plant. Include: (1) what it does, (2) how it works in simple terms, (3) one visible feature the visitor can see, (4) at least two key numbers, and (5) how it fits into the process sequence. Practise aloud at least three times. Bring the description to class ready to present in three minutes.

Professional Proposals

Learn the key components of a professional proposal — and what makes one persuasive enough to win a "yes".

Warm-Up

Warm-Up — What Is a Proposal?

Discuss these questions, then reveal the model answers.

1. When do professionals need to write a proposal?
When they want a client, a company or an investor to approve a project, fund an idea, or choose their service over a competitor's.
2. Who reads a proposal?
Busy decision-makers: managers, clients, board members. They often decide in minutes — so the proposal must be clear and quick to scan.
3. What is the difference between a proposal and a report?
A report informs. A proposal persuades. A proposal must convince the reader to say "yes" — to approve, fund, or buy.
4. What happens if a proposal is unclear or full of errors?
The reader loses trust and rejects it — even if the idea itself is excellent. Professional presentation is part of the message.
Useful phrases:
We propose to...This will result in...We would be delighted to...We look forward to your response.The total investment is...

Reading

A Real Proposal

Read this proposal. What is the writer asking for? How does the structure help the reader?

Proposal: In-Company Management Training Programme
Prepared for: Aurora Systems AG  ·  Prepared by: Articulate English  ·  Date: 10 August 2026
1. Executive Summary — We propose a 12-week management training programme for ten new team leaders at Aurora Systems. Ten participants will receive two 90-minute online sessions per week, led by a certified management trainer. The total cost is 4,800 EUR, all taxes included. The programme is designed to strengthen the leadership, delegation and feedback skills new managers need to run their teams effectively.
2. Background & Problem — Aurora Systems has promoted twelve employees into team leader roles over the past two years. An internal review shows that 7 out of 10 new managers feel unprepared for their responsibilities, and two teams missed their targets last year because of unclear goals and poor communication. Most new managers have never received formal management training.
3. Proposed Solution — We will deliver a structured programme combining role-play, live case studies and targeted feedback. Each week includes two 90-minute sessions: the first introduces new management concepts and techniques, the second applies them in realistic workplace scenarios. Every participant receives an individual progress report, and the programme ends with a recorded leadership simulation.
4. Timeline — The programme runs from 7 September to 27 November 2026 in three phases:
  • Weeks 1–2: needs analysis and skills assessment
  • Weeks 3–10: eight weeks of sessions
  • Weeks 11–12: final assessment and written report
A progress review is held with the team leader every four weeks.
5. Budget — Training fees 3,600 EUR; materials and platform licence 1,200 EUR. Total: 4,800 EUR, all taxes included.
6. Call to Action — We would be delighted to present this proposal at your next team meeting. We request a decision by 28 August so that the programme can begin in September.
The writer is asking the company to approve and fund a training programme. The structure helps because every section has one clear job: the summary sells the idea, the problem proves it is needed, the solution and timeline show it is feasible, the budget shows it is affordable, and the call to action tells the reader exactly what to do next.
Key Component 1

Executive Summary

What it is: A short overview of the whole proposal — the problem, the solution, the cost, and the benefit. Usually the first section the reader sees.

Why it matters: Decision-makers read it first. If the summary is weak, the rest of the proposal may never be read.

Example: "We propose a 12-week management programme for 10 team leaders at a cost of 4,800 EUR, designed to improve leadership skills and increase team performance."

A good summary can be written last — but it must always appear first. It should answer four questions in four sentences: What? For whom? How much? Why does it matter?
Key Component 2

Background & Problem

What it is: A clear description of the current situation and the problem the proposal solves. Include facts, figures and evidence.

Why it matters: It shows you understand the reader's world — not just your own service. A problem the reader recognises creates a need.

Example: "An internal review shows that 7 out of 10 new managers feel unprepared for their role, and two teams missed their targets last year because of unclear goals."

Use data. "Our sales are low" is weak and forgettable. "We lost two contracts worth 120,000 EUR" is specific, credible, and urgent. The reader must feel the problem is tangible.
Key Component 3

Proposed Solution

What it is: Your answer to the problem: exactly what you will do, for whom, and how it solves the issue.

Why it matters: This is the heart of the proposal. The reader wants a concrete plan — not vague promises like "we will improve things".

Example: "Two 90-minute case-study sessions per week, focused on delegation, feedback and performance reviews, delivered by a certified management trainer."

Be specific: what, who, how often, for how long, with whom. If a competitor could copy your sentence and it would still be true, your solution is too generic.
Key Component 4

Methodology & Timeline

What it is: How you will deliver the work and when — broken into phases, weeks or milestones.

Why it matters: A realistic timeline proves the project is feasible and shows you have thought about delivery, not just the idea.

Example: "Phase 1 (weeks 1–2): needs analysis and skills assessment. Phase 2 (weeks 3–10): eight weeks of sessions. Phase 3 (weeks 11–12): final assessment and report."

Vague deadlines ("as soon as possible") signal risk. Name weeks and milestones. A timeline also makes the budget believable — the reader can see what they are paying for at every stage.
Key Component 5

Budget

What it is: A clear, itemised list of all costs — and, ideally, what the money buys.

Why it matters: Hidden or unclear costs are the fastest way to lose a client's trust. Honest numbers build credibility.

Example: "Training fees 3,600 EUR, materials 600 EUR, platform licence 600 EUR. Total: 4,800 EUR, all taxes included."

Show value, not just price: "4,800 EUR for a programme that protects two contracts worth 120,000 EUR." A proposal that links cost to benefit is far more persuasive.
Key Component 6

Qualifications & Call to Action

What it is: Why you are the right person or team to deliver — followed by a clear request for the next step.

Why it matters: The reader must trust the team, and then know exactly what to do. A proposal without a call to action is just a document.

Example: "We have delivered over 200 hours of management training for three Swiss companies. We would be pleased to present this proposal at your next team meeting."

Use proof, not adjectives: "200 hours delivered" beats "we are experienced". End with one clear request — a meeting, a decision date, or a signature — and make it easy to say yes to.
Analysis

What Makes a Proposal Good?

A strong proposal passes these six tests. Which ones would your proposal pass?

Reader-focused — written for the decision-maker, not for the writer.
Specific — uses numbers, names, dates and concrete details.
Evidence-based — every claim is supported by facts or examples.
Feasible — the timeline and budget are realistic.
Persuasive — clear benefits, confident tone, no weak language.
Professional — clean structure, no errors, nothing vague.
Test every section with two questions: "So what?" and "Why should I believe you?". If a sentence cannot answer both, it does not belong in the proposal. And remember: your reader will probably spend 60 seconds on the first page — make those seconds count.
Practice

Weak vs Strong

Improve these weak proposal sentences.

1. Weak: "We offer good training at fair prices. Please contact us." — What makes this weak?
It is generic (anyone could say it), it has no audience, no evidence, no measurable outcome, and no clear next step. It answers neither "so what?" nor "why should I believe you?".
2. Rewrite: "We can help you improve your English."
"We will help your sales team negotiate international contracts with more confidence through two structured lessons per week, with measurable progress reported after week 12."
3. Which components are missing here? "We propose a programme. It will cost 4,800 EUR."
The problem, the solution details, the timeline, the qualifications and the call to action are all missing. The reader has no reason to say yes and no instruction on what to do.

Homework

Write a one-page proposal outline for an idea of your own — a new app, a school club, a training course, or a company project. Use all six components: executive summary, background & problem, solution, timeline, budget, and call to action. Bring it to class ready to present in two minutes.

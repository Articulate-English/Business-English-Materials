Plan, structure and deliver a presentation that keeps your audience with you — from the opening line to the final Q&A.

Warm-Up

Warm-Up — What Is a Good Presentation?

Discuss these questions, then reveal the model answers.

1. When do professionals give presentations?
Sales pitches, project updates, quarterly results, training sessions, product launches, conferences. In many Swiss companies, a presentation to the board or a client is a career-defining moment.
2. What makes an audience stop listening?
Reading slides word for word, talking too long without structure, mumbling, too much detail, no clear message. The audience switches off when they cannot see where the presentation is going.
3. What is the difference between a presentation and a written report?
A report is read and re-read; a presentation is heard once. It must be simpler, clearer and more repetitive — the audience cannot scroll back to check a slide.
4. What should the first 30 seconds achieve?
Win attention, introduce yourself, and state the one key message. If the opening fails, the audience spends the rest of the talk trying to catch up.
Useful phrases:
Good morning, thank you for coming.I'm here today to talk about...By the end of this presentation, you will know...Let me start with a quick story.Feel free to interrupt with questions.

A Model Presentation Opening

Read this opening. What does the speaker do in the first 30 seconds? Why does it work?

Opening: Customer Service Results — H1 Review
Speaker: Laura Keller, Head of Sales, Aurora Systems AG  ·  Audience: management board  ·  Duration: 15 minutes
1. Hook — "Good morning, everyone, and thank you for coming. Let me start with the one number that matters most: in the last six months, our customer satisfaction score rose twelve points. That is the fastest improvement in our history."
2. Introduction — "My name is Laura Keller, and I lead the sales team. I'm here today to talk about what we achieved, where we lost ground, and what we propose to do about it."
3. Key message — "By the end of this presentation, you will know three things: how we improved, why one region is still falling behind, and which two changes we want to make before October."
4. Agenda — "The presentation will take about fifteen minutes. I will keep it to three parts — results, causes, and next steps — and I will leave five minutes at the end for your questions."
5. Transition — "Let me start with the results."
In the first 30 seconds the speaker does five things: hooks the audience with a concrete number, introduces herself, states the topic, gives the key message ("three things"), and sets the agenda with a clear time plan. The audience knows exactly what is coming, why it matters, and how long it will take — so they can relax and listen.
Key Component 1

The Opening

What it is: The first 30–60 seconds of the talk: a hook, your name and role, the topic, the key message, and a short agenda.

Why it matters: Audiences decide in the first minute whether to listen. A strong opening tells them this talk is worth their time.

Example: "In the last six months, our customer satisfaction score rose twelve points. I'm Laura Keller, and I'll explain how we did it — and what we plan next."

Use a hook that fits your audience: a surprising number, a short story, or a direct question. Never start with "Sorry, I haven't prepared much..." — apologising wastes the only moment everyone is listening. Practice the first 30 seconds until they are automatic.
Key Component 2

Structure & Signposting

What it is: A clear order of sections, held together with signposts: "First...", "Now let's move on to...", "Finally...".

Why it matters: The audience cannot re-read your talk. Signposts let them follow — and they let you pause, find your place, and continue without losing anyone.

Example: "Now that we have looked at the results, let's turn to the causes behind them."

Use a simple three-part structure — problem, solution, benefit, or past, present, future. Announce the structure in the opening, then repeat it at every transition. If someone asks a question mid-talk, signpost your way back: "Good question — I'll come back to that. Now, back to the results."
Key Component 3

Slides & Visuals

What it is: Few slides, one idea per slide, and visuals that carry the message — charts, images, short headlines.

Why it matters: People read slides instead of listening. If a slide needs a full paragraph, the audience is reading while you talk — and you are competing with yourself.

Example: A slide with one chart, a title like "Satisfaction rose 12 points in H1", and nothing else.

Rule of thumb: maximum six lines per slide and six words per line. Never read the slide aloud. If a slide is too complex for the audience to grasp in five seconds, the detail belongs in a handout, not on the screen.
Key Component 4

Body Language & Voice

What it is: Eye contact, an open posture, steady volume, a clear pace, and pauses at the right moments.

Why it matters: A large part of your message is non-verbal. A speaker who fidgets, mumbles or reads from notes distracts from the content — however good it is.

Example: Pause for two seconds after your key message. Slow down when you say an important number.

Record yourself once and watch it. It is uncomfortable, but it fixes more bad habits than any book: filler words like "um" and "like", pacing, and nervous movements. Stand with your feet planted, keep your hands visible, and look at the audience — not at the screen.
Key Component 5

Handling Nerves & Q&A

What it is: Preparation for anxiety and a calm routine for the question round at the end.

Why it matters: Nerves are normal — even professional speakers feel them. The audience wants you to succeed, and a speaker who handles questions well looks competent.

Example: "That's a good question. Let me think about it for a moment." — buying time is professional, not weak.

Prepare the three hardest questions you expect and rehearse the answers. If you do not know something, say so: "I don't have that figure here, but I'll send it to you by tomorrow." Never invent numbers. Breathe slowly before you start — adrenaline makes you speak too fast.
Key Component 6

The Closing & Call to Action

What it is: A summary of the key message, one clear next step, a thank you, and the handover to questions.

Why it matters: The final words are what the audience remembers. A presentation without a clear closing ends in silence — and the message disappears.

Example: "To sum up: our results are strong, we know where we lost ground, and we propose to act before October. I'd like to invite you to the planning meeting next Tuesday."

Never end with "That's it" or "So, yeah". Plan the last sentence and practise it. Restate the key message, give one concrete next step, and then hand over: "Thank you for listening — I'm happy to take your questions now."
Analysis

What Makes a Presentation Good?

A strong presentation passes these six tests. Which ones would yours pass?

Clear — one key message the audience can repeat the next day.
Structured — obvious sections, held together by signposts.
Visual — slides support the talk instead of repeating it.
Confident — voice, pace and posture project certainty.
Audience-aware — language and detail fit the listeners.
Memorable — a story, a number, or a strong closing line.
Test your presentation with one question: "What will the audience remember tomorrow?" If the answer is nothing, the talk failed — regardless of how good the slides were. Aim for one number, one story, or one sentence they can repeat.
Practice

Weak vs Strong

Improve these weak presentation moments.

1. Weak opening: "Hi everyone, sorry I'm a bit nervous, so this might not be great. So, yeah, let's start." — What makes this weak?
Apologising kills credibility before the talk begins, "so, yeah" is empty filler, and there is no hook, no topic and no key message. The audience starts with low expectations — and low attention.
2. Rewrite the opening: "Good morning — by the end of these ten minutes you'll know exactly why our satisfaction score rose twelve points, and what we plan to do next."
That version is strong: it greets the audience, promises a concrete outcome, gives a number, and sets the structure. No apology, no filler, no vague topic.
3. A slide contains 60 words of full sentences. What is wrong with it?
The audience reads instead of listening, the speaker is tempted to read it aloud, and nothing is memorable. Fix it: one headline, one chart, one takeaway sentence. Move the detail to a handout.
